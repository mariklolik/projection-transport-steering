\title{What the Decision Buys:\\ An Exact Account of Conditional Activation Steering}
\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Conditional activation steering edits a language model only on inputs that a
decision flags, and it is usually shown to beat steering every input. Such
comparisons leave open how much of the gain comes from the decision and how
much from the edit. We give an exact account: the selectivity of any
conditional edit equals the decision's error rates weighted by the rates at
which the edit converts the inputs it flags. The account predicts gated edits
from a single ungated run, states when steering can beat overriding the
behavior's readout with the same decision, and calls for two null arms that
pass the same decision to no edit and to a random edit. We apply it to
answer-level overconfidence with Probe-Gated Steering (PGS), a correctness
probe in front of a projection edit. In pre-registered comparisons on two model
families and two benchmarks, PGS exceeds its no-edit null in every setting and
its random-edit null in two of three, and it is the most selective of eight
methods on Gemma-MMLU while lowering calibration error. The decision carries
most of the effect, which also transfers to sycophancy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Language models carry many behaviors along directions of their activations,
and activation steering changes a behavior by moving the activation along such
a direction~\citep{turner2023actadd,rimsky2024caa,zou2023repe,li2023iti,arditi2024refusal}.
The edit costs one vector operation per token and is applied to every input, so
a dose that removes a behavior also changes the inputs that never showed it.
Conditional methods place a decision in front of the edit:
CAST~\citep{lee2024cast} steers only when the prompt resembles a condition
vector, and PCHI~\citep{liu2026probe} rescales attention heads only when a
probe flags a wrong but confident answer. Both are compared with global
steering, and both improve on it.

A conditional edit makes two choices, which inputs to edit and how to edit
them, and a comparison with global steering credits the pair. We study
overconfidence in multiple-choice reasoning, where the answers to change are
the ones the model gets wrong while stating that it is right, and the answers
to protect are the ones it gets right and says so. On
\texttt{gemma-2-2b-it}, the additive dose that removes every
overconfident-wrong answer also removes every confident-right one
(Sec.~\ref{ssec:confirm}), so a decision is needed, and the open question is
how much of the result it supplies.

We answer it with an identity. The selectivity of any conditional edit equals
the decision's true-positive rate times the rate at which the edit converts
the flagged target answers, minus its false-positive rate times the rate at
which it converts the flagged protected ones (Prop.~\ref{prop:account}). The
identity gives three references that a conditional method can be held to: the
same decision with no edit, which measures how often re-generation alone
changes an answer; the same decision with a random edit of the same size; and
the same decision overriding the behavior's readout, which converts every
flagged answer. It also states in advance when steering beats the last of
these (Eq.~\ref{eq:override}). We instantiate the account as Probe-Gated
Steering (PGS): a logistic-regression probe, the estimate of the likelihood
ratio that Theorem~\ref{thm:gate} shows to be the optimal decision, in front of
a projection-local edit of the confidence direction.

Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item An accounting identity for conditional edits, the condition under which
steering is more selective than detect-and-override, and a Neyman--Pearson
characterization of the optimal decision (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}). The
identity predicts the selectivity of 480 gated configurations from ungated
runs (correlation $0.97$ on Gemma, $0.91$ on Qwen) and composes the
confirmatory gated arms of three settings to within $0.025$
(Sec.~\ref{ssec:law}).
\item A pre-registered comparison in which every method is tuned under one rule
and run once on unseen questions, for two model families and a second
benchmark, with null arms registered and timestamped before they were run.
PGS is the most selective of eight methods on Gemma-MMLU, significantly so
against all seven baselines, and it lowers expected calibration error there by
$0.10$ and on ARC by $0.06$ (Sec.~\ref{ssec:confirm}).
\item A decomposition of what the decision buys. Passing a random direction
through the probe recovers $73\%$ of the selectivity of PGS on Gemma, and
overriding the readout with the probe recovers all of it; across five
decisions the override reference follows the probe's held-out AUROC, and the
same account applies to sycophancy (Sec.~\ref{ssec:decides}).
\item A one-pass form of the decision, read from the prompt or a short prefix,
that matches the post-hoc decision on Gemma (Sec.~\ref{ssec:onepass-results}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Global steering.} ActAdd~\citep{turner2023actadd} and
CAA~\citep{rimsky2024caa} add a contrastive direction to the residual stream,
ITI~\citep{li2023iti} shifts probe-selected attention heads,
RepE~\citep{zou2023repe} reads and writes representation directions,
directional ablation~\citep{arditi2024refusal} projects a direction out, and
SAE clamping~\citep{templeton2024scaling} pins a feature. The same edit reaches
every input, and AxBench~\citep{wu2025axbench} and studies of steering
reliability~\citep{tan2024generalisation,braun2025unreliability} find such
edits brittle across inputs. MiMiC~\citep{singh2024mimic} derives the Gaussian
optimal-transport map between two activation distributions,
Linear-AcT~\citep{rodriguez2025act} transports each neuron with a univariate
map, LinEAS~\citep{rodriguez2025lineas} learns affine maps end to end, and
COAST~\citep{nguyen2026coast} and A-LQR~\citep{nguyen2026alqr} minimise
collateral change for a given target. These works choose the action; we keep
the action simple and measure what the decision adds.

\textbf{Conditional steering.} CAST~\citep{lee2024cast} applies a behavior
vector only when the prompt's hidden state is similar to a condition vector,
and is the baseline closest to our design; we reimplement its prompt condition
and give it the same labelled data as our probe. Dynamic and
semantics-adaptive methods scale the edit per
token~\citep{ferrando2026dsas,wang2025sadi,scalena2024dynamic}, and
GAPS~\citep{gaps2026} gates individual dimensions. Closest in aim is
PCHI~\citep{liu2026probe}, which also separates detection from intervention
for verbalised overconfidence: a linear probe on the confidence template
conditions a learned rescaling of attention heads. PCHI learns the action for
a fixed readout position; we fix the action and ask what the decision
contributes, when it can be taken, and against which references it should be
judged. Probes read after a few generated tokens predict whether steering will
succeed~\citep{steerable2026}, and detectors of a finished behavior forecast it
less well from a prefix~\citep{fpcg2026}; we measure this prefix cost for our
decision directly.

\textbf{Correctness probes.} That a model's activations predict whether its
answer is right is well established~\citep{kadavath2022know,azaria2023lying},
and reading a direction off already-edited activations degrades
it~\citep{luo2026yopo}. Our override reference is selective prediction with
such a probe, and the identity turns it into the baseline that a conditional
edit of a confidence readout has to beat.

\section{Probe-Gated Steering}
\label{sec:method}

A conditional edit makes two choices: how to change an activation, and on which
inputs to change it. We call the first the \emph{action} and the second the
\emph{decision}. This section describes the family of actions
(Sec.~\ref{ssec:action}), writes selectivity as a function of the two choices
and derives the references (Sec.~\ref{ssec:decision}), and moves the decision
into a single pass (Sec.~\ref{ssec:onepass}). Proofs are in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\textbf{Setup.} Let $h\in\mathbb{R}^d$ be the residual-stream activation at a
fixed layer and $V\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}$ a matrix with orthonormal rows
that spans a behavioral subspace. We write $\mu_V$ for the law of the
projection $Vh$ under unsteered generation and $\nu$ for a target law on
$\mathbb{R}^k$. In our experiments $k=1$ and $V=\hat v^\top$ is the confidence
direction, the difference of mean activations between confident and
nonconfident answers on an extraction split (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}).

\subsection{The action}
\label{ssec:action}

Large edits are what damage generation (Appendix~\ref{app:capability}), so we
restrict the action to the behavioral subspace. An edit $T$ is \emph{local} to
$V$ if $(I-V^\top V)\,T(h)=(I-V^\top V)\,h$, and its cost is
$\mathbb{E}\lVert T(h)-h\rVert^2$.

\begin{proposition}[local edits]
\label{prop:ot}
Let $\mu_V$ be absolutely continuous and let $\mu_V,\nu$ have finite second
moments. Among local edits under which $Vh$ has law $\nu$, the cost is
minimised by $T(h)=h+V^\top\big(S(Vh)-Vh\big)$, where $S$ is the Brenier map
from $\mu_V$ to $\nu$, and the minimum is $\Wtwo^2(\mu_V,\nu)$.
\end{proposition}

The result is Brenier's theorem applied to the projection, and we use it only
to describe the family. For $k=1$, $S$ is the monotone
rearrangement of quantiles; ablation is the map onto $\delta_0$, a clamp maps
onto a truncated law, and the shift $S(p)=p+\alpha$ maps onto a translate of
$\mu_V$. The tuning grid of every setting contains all three kinds of map, and
the selection rule picks a shift or ablation each time
(Appendix~\ref{app:tuning}). The reason is visible in the data: on Gemma the
law of the confidence projection over calibrated answers differs from its
unsteered law by $0.03$ standard deviations in mean and $4\%$ in spread, so the
transport map onto it barely moves an activation, whereas the selected shift moves the projection
by $3.2$ standard deviations.

\subsection{The decision and its references}
\label{ssec:decision}

A decision reads a statistic $s$ of the input and runs the action on the event
$\{s>\tau\}$; elsewhere the output is the unsteered one. We measure the result
with selectivity,
$\mathrm{Sel}=P(\text{leave }\ocw\mid\ocw)-P(\text{leave }\crok\mid\crok)$, the
share of overconfident-wrong answers (\ocw{}) the edit removes minus the share
of confident-right answers (\crok{}) it destroys. Write $\mathrm{TPR}$ and
$\mathrm{FPR}$ for the rates at which the decision fires on \ocw{} and \crok{},
and $\rho_O$, $\rho_C$ for the rates at which the action changes the state of a
flagged \ocw{} and a flagged \crok{} answer.

\begin{proposition}[selectivity accounting]
\label{prop:account}
For any decision and action that leave unflagged outputs unchanged,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Sel}=\mathrm{TPR}\cdot\rho_O-\mathrm{FPR}\cdot\rho_C .
\label{eq:account}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:account} separates the two choices, and each of its terms has a
reference arm. If the action converts flagged and unflagged answers of the same
state at the same rate, $\rho_O$ and $\rho_C$ can be read off one ungated run,
and the equation predicts the selectivity of every decision placed in front of
that action. Re-generating the flagged answers without an edit gives
$\rho_O,\rho_C>0$ through decoding nondeterminism alone, so the same decision
with an identity action is the \emph{no-edit null}; the same decision with a
norm-matched random direction is the \emph{random-edit null}, and it measures
what any perturbation of that size achieves. Overriding the reported confidence
of every flagged answer gives $\rho_O=\rho_C=1$ and reaches
$\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR}$. A gated edit is more selective than this
\emph{detect-and-override} reference exactly when
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{TPR}\,(1-\rho_O)<\mathrm{FPR}\,(1-\rho_C),
\label{eq:override}
\end{equation}
that is, when the action spares the confident-right answers the decision
flags by mistake more often than it fails on the overconfident-wrong answers
it flags correctly. How large the decision term can be depends on how well $s$
separates the two states.

\begin{theorem}[optimal decision and its ceiling]
\label{thm:gate}
Suppose that within each state the action's conversion probability does not
depend on $s$. (a) For any objective increasing in TPR and decreasing in FPR,
including Eq.~\eqref{eq:account}, thresholding the likelihood ratio of $s$
between \ocw{} and \crok{} is optimal at every operating point. Without the
assumption, the optimal decision thresholds the conversion-weighted ratio
$p_O(s)\,r_O(s)/\big(p_C(s)\,r_C(s)\big)$, where $p$ is the class-conditional
density of $s$ and $r(s)$ the conversion probability given $s$. (b) If the
concave hull of the ROC of $s$ has area $\mathrm{AUC}$, then
$\max_\tau(\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR})\le2\,\mathrm{AUC}-1$, which bounds the
detect-and-override reference and any gated edit with $\rho_O=\rho_C$.
\end{theorem}

We therefore implement the decision as a logistic-regression probe, an estimate
of the log-likelihood ratio, fitted on answers labelled by the evaluation
readout and judged by its AUROC on questions it was not fitted to. The same
bound explains why the decision should not read the coordinate the action
edits: on held-out questions the confidence projection separates \ocw{} from
\crok{} at AUROC $0.64$ for Gemma and $0.53$ for Qwen.

\subsection{Deciding in one pass}
\label{ssec:onepass}

A decision that reads the finished answer requires generating every flagged
question twice. The decision can instead read the prompt, or the mean
activation over the first $t$ generated tokens, with a probe fitted on exactly
that statistic. Theorem~\ref{thm:gate} applies to any such statistic, so the
cost of deciding early is the AUROC lost relative to the finished answer. The
reader sits at or above the layer the action writes, the action starts at the
decision token, and positions written before it keep their cached keys. The
operator then costs two $d$-dimensional dot products per token.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp}\label{sec:data}

\textbf{Models and task.} The main model is \texttt{gemma-2-2b-it}
\citep{gemma2024}; the confirmatory comparison is repeated on
\texttt{Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct}, a second model family, with its own directions,
probes and tuning. Both reason inside \texttt{<think>...</think>} and answer
\verb|\boxed{letter}| on MMLU~\citep{hendrycks2021mmlu}; decoding is greedy.
The action is applied at layer 14 of 26 (Gemma) and 14 of 28 (Qwen). A
replication on ARC-Challenge~\citep{clark2018arc} reuses the Gemma directions,
refits every decision on ARC training questions, and follows the same protocol
with its own tuning and confirmatory splits (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\textbf{Splits.} All splits are disjoint sets of MMLU test questions (ids in
the released code): an \emph{extraction} split of 400 questions for the
directions, a \emph{detector} pool of 4{,}000 for the probes, a \emph{tuning}
split of 1{,}200 on which every method's configuration is chosen, and a
\emph{confirmatory} split of 3{,}000 used once, under a protocol whose hashes
were committed before its steered rollouts were generated
(Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\textbf{What is measured.} The primary readout (M5) scores the answer the
model actually generates. Correctness is the \verb|\boxed{}| letter against
the gold answer; confidence is $P(\mathrm{YES})$ when the model is then asked,
in the same conversation, whether that answer is correct. A steered question is
steered for both the reasoning trace and the confidence query. Each question is
in one of four states: \emph{overconfident-wrong} (\ocw{}, wrong with
$P(\mathrm{YES})\ge0.5$), nonconfident-wrong, nonconfident-right, and
\emph{confident-right} (\crok{}). Two secondary readouts, the letter-logit
confidence of the same answer and a per-option verification readout, are
reported in Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}; the prompts are in
Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.

\textbf{Metrics.} Pairing each question with itself before and after the edit,
removal is $P(\text{not }\ocw\text{ after}\mid\ocw\text{ before})$, retention
is $P(\crok\text{ after}\mid\crok\text{ before})$, and
$\mathrm{Sel}=\text{removal}-(1-\text{retention})$. Because Sel counts only
answers that start in \ocw{} or \crok{}, we also report the net change in the
share of \ocw{} answers, $\Delta$ECE (10 bins) and $\Delta$Brier of the stated
confidence over all questions, and $\Delta$acc.

\textbf{Methods and tuning.} We compare prompting (a calibration instruction),
additive CAA~\citep{rimsky2024caa}, directional
ablation~\citep{arditi2024refusal}, MiMiC~\citep{singh2024mimic},
Linear-AcT~\citep{rodriguez2025act} and two conditional shifts: CAST with its
original prompt condition, fitted on the same labelled pool as our
probe~\citep{lee2024cast}, and a CAST-style variant conditioned on the finished
trace's projection onto an \ocw{}--\crok{} direction. All are reimplemented on
the same hooks, records and readouts. The PGS probe is a logistic regression on
the mean activation of one layer, with layer and regularisation chosen by AUROC
on the tuning split. Each method searches a grid of 8 to 16 configurations on
the tuning split, and one rule selects all of them: the highest selectivity
among configurations whose $\Delta$acc is at least $-0.01$
(Appendix~\ref{app:tuning}). Post-hoc PGS reads the finished unsteered answer
and regenerates the flagged questions; single-pass PGS decides at the prompt,
or after $t\in\{16,32,64,128\}$ generated tokens, and acts from then on.

\textbf{Null arms and statistics.} On each confirmatory split we run the frozen
PGS decision with an identity action (no-edit null) and with the selected
action applied to a unit random direction (random-edit null). Their protocol
was pushed to a public repository before they were run. The unit of analysis
is the question; intervals are paired bootstrap intervals with $10^4$
resamples. The seven comparisons of post-hoc PGS against the baselines are
Holm-adjusted within each setting, and the six comparisons against the null
arms are Holm-adjusted together. An accuracy change is non-inferior when the
lower end of its $95\%$ interval is above $-0.02$.

\subsection{The identity predicts gated edits}
\label{ssec:law}

On the Gemma tuning split we measure the conversion rates of six actions once,
compose each with two decisions at 20 thresholds, and compare the prediction
$\mathrm{TPR}\,\rho_O-\mathrm{FPR}\,\rho_C$ with the composed selectivity
(Fig.~\ref{fig:law}a). The two agree with correlation $0.97$ over 240
combinations, and with correlation $0.91$ over the 240 Qwen combinations,
whose selectivities span a narrower range. The prediction is higher by $0.033$
on average for Gemma and $0.012$ for Qwen, because the answers a decision
flags are converted more often than the average answer of their state, and
more so in the protected state; with the flagged conversion rates in place of
the ungated ones the identity is exact. Because a post-hoc gated arm is,
question by question, either its unsteered or its ungated output, its
selectivity can be composed from one ungated run. On the confirmatory splits,
twelve real gated arms across the three settings land within $0.025$ of their
composition, with a median difference of $0.005$ (Appendix~\ref{app:law});
this is how gated configurations are scored during tuning.

The identity also says what each choice contributes. The shift selected for
Gemma, $S(p)=p-0.375\lVert h\rVert$, changes the state of $84\%$ of
overconfident-wrong and $59\%$ of confident-right answers when applied
everywhere. A decision therefore recovers $0.59$ for every confident-right
answer it leaves alone and forfeits $0.84$ for every overconfident-wrong answer
it misses, so the value of detection grows with how indiscriminate the action
is. The probe reads the unsteered answer at held-out AUROC $0.74$, against
$0.67$ for the direction that conditions the CAST-style baseline
(Fig.~\ref{fig:law}b).

reference. The override reference rises with the held-out AUROC of the
decision, consistent with the ceiling of Theorem~\ref{thm:gate}: on Gemma it

$[0.393, 0.472]$ while lowering ECE from $0.216$ to $0.139$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:override}
says when a gated edit can exceed its override, and the data follow it. On
Gemma the frozen shift converts $91\%$ of flagged overconfident-wrong and
$89\%$ of flagged confident-right answers, so steering with the probe comes
close to its override without passing it. On ARC the difference-of-means
direction fitted on 400 labels fires on $77\%$ of confident-right answers, and
the shift spares most of them; steering then reaches $0.297$ against $0.134$
for the override with the same decision.

The account transfers to a second behavior. In a sycophancy setting on the
Gemma confirmatory questions, the user disputes the model's first answer and
the model either caves or holds; a probe on the first answer's trace predicts
its correctness at AUROC $0.72$. Keeping the first answer whenever the probe
judges it correct reaches selectivity $0.311$ $[0.277, 0.345]$ between caving
and accepting a correction, at an accuracy gain of $17.5$ points, against
$0.096$ for the best gated deference edit (Appendix~\ref{app:sycophancy}).

\subsection{Where the edit acts}
\label{ssec:where}

We reran the frozen PGS decision with the action applied only during the
reasoning trace, or only during the turn in which the model states its
confidence. On Gemma, where the action is a shift, steering only the
confidence turn reaches selectivity $0.274$ $[0.237, 0.312]$, close to the
$0.294$ of the full edit, and steering only the trace reaches $0.159$. On Qwen,
where the action is ablation, the pattern reverses: the trace-only edit reaches
$0.172$, equal to the full edit, and the confidence-turn edit $0.006$. A shift
of the confidence coordinate therefore acts mainly on the stated confidence,
while ablation acts through the reasoning. The steered traces also change
answers: on Gemma PGS turns $124$ of the $963$ overconfident-wrong answers into
correct ones and $106$ of the $1{,}399$ confident-right answers into wrong ones
(Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}).

\subsection{Deciding in one pass}
\label{ssec:onepass-results}

Most of the detection is available before the answer is written.
Fig.~\ref{fig:law}b plots the held-out AUROC of a probe fitted on each
statistic. For Gemma the prompt alone reaches $0.720$ and the first 16 tokens
$0.720$, against $0.741$ for the finished answer; for Qwen the prompt reaches
$0.761$ against $0.785$. On Gemma the single-pass operators in
Table~\ref{tab:confirm} match the post-hoc one: deciding at token 32 gives
selectivity $0.294$ and lowers ECE by $0.136$, and deciding at the prompt gives
$0.306$, with an accuracy interval that reaches the margin. A decision at the prompt costs one dot product on an activation the
forward pass already computes, so the operator adds no generation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented an exact account of conditional activation steering and
Probe-Gated Steering, the operator it leads to. The account changes how a
conditional edit should be evaluated: next to global steering, it should be
compared with the same decision passed to no edit, to a random edit, and to an
override of the behavior's readout, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:override} says in
advance when it can beat the last. Under a pre-registered protocol, PGS was
the most selective of eight methods on Gemma-MMLU with a net reduction in
calibration error, exceeded its no-edit null in all three settings, and most of
what it bought came from the decision.

Our study has several limitations. First, the evidence covers overconfidence
and, in exploratory form, sycophancy, on two instruction-tuned models of up to
7B parameters and two multiple-choice benchmarks. Second, on Qwen the edit
removes overconfident-wrong answers about as often as it creates new ones, so
the net calibration of that model does not change, and a calibration prompt is
as selective there and on ARC. Third, the decision is a probe trained on
labelled answers, and its threshold is tied to the distribution it was fitted
on (Appendices~\ref{app:capability} and~\ref{app:transfer}). Where the
behavior has a readout that can be overwritten, the override reference was at
least as selective as steering with the same probe; for behaviors expressed in
free text, the action's conversion rates become the whole question, and the
identity measures them directly.

\textbf{Splits.} All splits are subsets of the 14{,}042-question MMLU test set,
identified by index and constructed by \texttt{label\_pool.split\_records}. The
extraction split (400) excludes the first evaluation subset of the pilot
protocol (Appendix~\ref{app:legacy}); the tuning split (1{,}200) excludes the
extraction split and the five pilot evaluation subsets; the detector pool (4{,}000) and the confirmatory
split (3{,}000) are consecutive blocks of a seeded permutation of the
remaining questions. No question appears in two of the four splits.

\textbf{Selection rule.} For every method the configuration with the highest
tuning-split selectivity among those with $\Delta\mathrm{acc}\ge-0.01$ is
carried unchanged to the confirmatory split. Gated configurations are scored on
the tuning split by combining the ungated rollouts of the same split with the
gate's flags (Prop.~\ref{prop:account} makes this exact up to batched-decoding
nondeterminism; Appendix~\ref{app:law}); single-pass configurations are real
runs. Every confirmatory number is a real run.

\textbf{Tests.} Primary: PGS post-hoc against each of the seven baselines on
pooled M5 selectivity, question-level paired bootstrap with $10^4$ resamples,
two-sided, Holm-adjusted over the seven comparisons. Accuracy: an arm is
non-inferior if the lower end of the $95\%$ interval of its accuracy change is
above $-0.02$. The single-pass and prompt-decision variants, M4 and M2
selectivities, removal, retention and ECE are secondary and reported without
a claim.

\textbf{Released records.} Every unsteered and steered answer of the tuning and
confirmatory splits is released with its state, confidence and correctness
(\texttt{results/release/records.csv.gz}); \texttt{paper/recompute\_from\_records.py}

and~\ref{tab:confirm2}, including the null arms, from that file alone.

\textbf{ARC replication.} A second protocol

\textbf{Null arms.} A third protocol (\texttt{confirmatory-v21-null.md}), with

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\textbf{Proposition~\ref{prop:ot}.} By locality, $T(h)-h\in\mathrm{span}(V^\top)$
$\mu$-a.s., hence $\lVert T(h)-h\rVert^2=\lVert VT(h)-Vh\rVert^2$ because the
rows of $V$ are orthonormal, and $C(T)=\mathbb{E}\lVert VT(h)-Vh\rVert^2$. The
joint law $\gamma$ of $(Vh,VT(h))$ is a coupling of $(\mu_V,\nu)$ by the goal
constraint, so $C(T)=\int\lVert y-x\rVert^2d\gamma\ge\Wtwo^2(\mu_V,\nu)$.
Because $\mu_V$ is absolutely continuous and both laws have finite second
moments, Brenier's theorem gives a unique optimal coupling, induced by
$S=\nabla\varphi$ with $\varphi$ convex. $T^*(h)=h+V^\top(S(Vh)-Vh)$ is
admissible ($VV^\top=I_k$ gives $VT^*(h)=S(Vh)$, and the complement is
untouched) and attains the bound. For any minimiser the induced coupling is
optimal, hence equal to the unique one, so $VT(h)=S(Vh)$ a.s.\ \qed

For $k=1$ absolute continuity can be weakened to atomlessness of $\mu_V$: the
monotone rearrangement $F_\nu^{-1}\circ F_{\mu_V}$ is then an optimal map for
every $\nu$ with a finite second moment, including targets with atoms such as
$\delta_0$ (ablation) or a law truncated at $c$ (clamp); uniqueness of the map
can fail only on a $\mu_V$-null set.

\begin{proposition}[metric covariance]
\label{prop:metric}
If the cost is $(\Delta h)^\top M(\Delta h)$ with $M\succ0$, the admissible
problem is optimal transport of $\mu_V$ to $\nu$ in $\mathbb{R}^k$ under the
cost $\lVert\cdot\rVert^2_{M_V}$, $M_V=VMV^\top\succ0$, and the optimal
operator is $T^*(h)=h+V^\top(S_M(Vh)-Vh)$ with
$S_M=M_V^{-1/2}\circ\nabla\varphi\circ M_V^{1/2}$, $\varphi$ convex.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof.} Locality writes $\Delta h=V^\top a$ with $a\in\mathbb{R}^k$, and
the goal constraint with $VV^\top=I_k$ fixes $a=S(Vh)-Vh$ for the map $S$ in
use. Then $\lVert\Delta h\rVert_M^2=a^\top M_Va$. The cost is a latent-space
Mahalanobis cost on $\mathbb{R}^k$; it is not the full-space Mahalanobis
distance of $\Delta h$ unless $M$ commutes with $V^\top V$. In the coordinates
$z=M_V^{1/2}q$ it is Euclidean, the change of variables is internal to
$\mathbb{R}^k$, and Brenier's theorem in $z$ (the pushforward of an absolutely
continuous law under an invertible linear map is absolutely continuous) gives
$\nabla\varphi$; mapping back gives $S_M$. \qed

\textbf{Shifts.} If $\nu=\mu_V*\delta_\alpha$ the
map $x\mapsto x+\alpha$ is the gradient of a convex function pushing $\mu_V$
to $\nu$, hence the Brenier map. Conversely, if the optimal map is
$x+\alpha$ then $\nu$ is its pushforward, the shift. For one-dimensional
Gaussians the monotone map is $x\mapsto m_t+(\sigma_t/\sigma_s)(x-m_s)$, with
slope $\sigma_t/\sigma_s$. If two groups of inputs have the same law of $p$
and the probability that an input changes state depends on $h$ only through
$p$, every transfer function changes both groups at the same rate, since both
rates are the same integral against the law of $p$. A decision that reads $p$
alone therefore adds little, which is why the probe reads the full activation.

\textbf{Proposition~\ref{prop:account}.} Let $R$ be the event that an \ocw{}
trace leaves \ocw{} and $L$ the event that a \crok{} trace leaves \crok{}. Off
$\Gamma$ the output equals the unsteered output, which is in its original state
by definition, so $R\subseteq\Gamma$ and $L\subseteq\Gamma$ up to null sets.
Then $P(R\mid\ocw)=P(\Gamma\mid\ocw)P(R\mid\ocw,\Gamma)=\mathrm{TPR}\,\rho_O$, and
likewise $P(L\mid\crok)=\mathrm{FPR}\,\rho_C$; subtracting gives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:account}. If, within \ocw{}, the indicator that the action
converts the trace is independent of $\Gamma$, then
$\rho_O=P(R^{\mathrm{ungated}}\mid\ocw)$, and likewise for \crok{}; the ungated
selectivity is $\rho_O-\rho_C$, and the difference is
$\rho_C(1-\mathrm{FPR})-\rho_O(1-\mathrm{TPR})$. \qed

The identity requires that the unflagged output be exactly the unsteered one.
The post-hoc gate satisfies this by construction. The single-pass gate
satisfies it for traces on which it never fires, since the action hook is the
identity until the decision token.

\textbf{Theorem~\ref{thm:gate}.} Write $p_O,p_C$ for the densities of $s$ on
\ocw{} and \crok{} answers and $r_O(s),r_C(s)$ for the probabilities that the
action converts an answer of each state with statistic $s$. For a decision
region $\Gamma$, $\mathrm{TPR}\,\rho_O=\int_\Gamma p_O r_O$ and
$\mathrm{FPR}\,\rho_C=\int_\Gamma p_C r_C$, so
$\mathrm{Sel}(\Gamma)=\int_\Gamma(p_O r_O-p_C r_C)$. Among regions with a given
value of $\int_\Gamma p_C r_C$, the Neyman--Pearson lemma applied to the
nonnegative measures $p_O r_O$ and $p_C r_C$ shows that
$\int_\Gamma p_O r_O$ is maximised by a superlevel set of their ratio, which is
the general statement in (a). If $r_O$ and $r_C$ are constant in $s$, the ratio
is a constant multiple of $p_O/p_C$, so every objective increasing in TPR and
decreasing in FPR is maximised on the likelihood-ratio ROC curve, which is (a).
The assumption matters: with $s\in\{a,b\}$, $p_O(a)=p_O(b)=\tfrac12$,
$p_C(a)=\tfrac14$, $p_C(b)=\tfrac34$, $r_O(a)=0.2$, $r_O(b)=1$, $r_C(a)=1$ and
$r_C(b)=0$, the likelihood ratio ranks $a$ above $b$, yet $\Gamma=\{b\}$ gives
selectivity $0.5$, against $-0.15$ for $\{a\}$ and $0.35$ for $\{a,b\}$. On
our data $r$ does depend on $s$: flagged answers are converted more often than
unflagged answers of the same state (Appendix~\ref{app:law}). Our detector is a
logistic-regression probe, a discriminative estimate of the log-likelihood
ratio up to the prior term; (a) states what the probe approximates, not that
the fitted probe is optimal. (b) The concavified ROC contains the path
$(0,0)\to(\mathrm{FPR}^*,\mathrm{TPR}^*)\to(1,1)$ through the Youden-optimal
point, whose area is $\tfrac12(1+J)$, so $J\le2\,\mathrm{AUC}-1$. The override
reference has selectivity $\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR}\le J$, and with
$\rho_O=\rho_C=\rho$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:account} gives $\mathrm{Sel}=\rho(\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR})\le J$. \qed

\begin{proposition}[transport under a fixed decision]
\label{prop:joint}
Among interventions local to $V$, equal to the identity off $\Gamma$, and whose
projections on $\Gamma$ follow $\nu_\Gamma$, the cost-minimal one applies the
Brenier map of $(\mu_V\mid\Gamma)\to\nu_\Gamma$ on $\Gamma$, at cost
$P(\Gamma)\,\Wtwo^2(\mu_V\!\mid\!\Gamma,\nu_\Gamma)$, provided
$\mu_V(\cdot\mid\Gamma)$ is absolutely continuous.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof.} Split $C(T)$ over $\Gamma$ and $\Gamma^c$. The second term is
zero by the identity constraint; the first, with the conditional goal
constraint, is Proposition~\ref{prop:ot} for $\mu(\cdot\mid\Gamma)$. \qed

This result fixes the decision. It does not say that a threshold gate is the
best decision for the displacement-priced objective; the next statement shows
that it generally is not, and when it is.

\begin{proposition}[graded dose]
\label{prop:dose}
Let $T_\lambda(h)=h+\lambda(s)\,V^\top(S(Vh)-Vh)$ with a dose
$\lambda:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]$ measurable in the detector statistic $s$. Write
$\pi(s)=P(\ocw\mid s)$, $d(s)=\mathbb{E}[\lVert S(Vh)-Vh\rVert^2\mid s]>0$, and
suppose the behavioral gain is linear in the dose, with slope $\beta_1>0$ on
\ocw{} and $-\beta_0<0$ on \crok{}. The dose maximising gain minus
displacement priced at $1/(2c)$ is
\[
\lambda^*(s)=\Big[\tfrac{c\,(\beta_0+\beta_1)\,(\pi(s)-\tau)}{d(s)}\Big]_0^1,\qquad
\tau=\tfrac{\beta_0}{\beta_0+\beta_1}.
\]
It is zero exactly on $\{\pi\le\tau\}$ and nondecreasing in the ratio
$(\pi(s)-\tau)/d(s)$. It is nondecreasing in $\pi$ when $d$ is constant, but
not in general. The threshold gate $\mathbf{1}[\pi>\tau]$ is optimal iff
$\lambda^*\in\{0,1\}$ almost surely, and strictly suboptimal iff
$P(0<\lambda^*<1)>0$.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof.} The displacement of $T_\lambda$ is
$\lambda(s)^2\lVert S(Vh)-Vh\rVert^2$, with expectation
$\mathbb{E}_s[\lambda(s)^2d(s)]$; the gain is
$\mathbb{E}_s[\lambda(s)((\beta_0+\beta_1)\pi(s)-\beta_0)]$. The objective is
an integral over $s$ of a strictly concave quadratic in $\lambda$, maximised
pointwise on $[0,1]$ by the clipped stationary point. It is the unique
maximiser a.s., so the indicator is optimal iff it agrees with $\lambda^*$
a.s., i.e.\ iff $\lambda^*\in\{0,1\}$ a.s.; otherwise the objective is strictly
larger at $\lambda^*$ on a set of positive measure. For non-monotonicity in
$\pi$, take $s_1,s_2$ with $\pi(s_1)=0.6<\pi(s_2)=0.7$, $\tau=0.5$,
$c(\beta_0+\beta_1)=1$, $d(s_1)=0.2$ and $d(s_2)=1$: then
$\lambda^*(s_1)=0.5>\lambda^*(s_2)=0.2$. For a counterexample to strict
suboptimality at every finite price, let $\pi$ take only the values $0.1$ and
$0.9$ with $\tau=0.5$ and $d\le0.4\,c(\beta_0+\beta_1)$: then
$\lambda^*\in\{0,1\}$ a.s.\ and the threshold gate is optimal. \qed

\textbf{Single-pass decision.} Let $\mathcal{F}_t$ be generated by the first $t$
generated tokens and $z_t$ the mean detector feature over them. A decision
measurable with respect to $\sigma(z_t)$ is a test between the
class-conditional laws of $z_t$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:gate} applies with $z_t$
in place of $s$ and nothing is assumed about how $z_t$ relates to the finished
trace. The reader hook sits at a layer at or above the actor; within one
forward pass the actor at position $t$ sees the decision accumulated over
positions $<t$, the running mean needs one sum and one counter per sequence,
and an edit at position $t$ changes only the keys and values written at $t$.

\begin{proposition}[computational cost]
\label{prop:compute}
Any algorithm implementing a non-degenerate intervention local to a subspace
spanned by a dense direction reads all $d$ coordinates of $h$, so it costs
$\Omega(d)$ per token. The edit of Proposition~\ref{prop:ot} costs $\Theta(kd+k\log K)$ with a
$K$-knot lookup, and the detector adds one $d$-dimensional dot product per
token.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof.} If a coordinate $i$ with $(\hat v)_i\neq0$ is never read, an
adversary perturbs $h_i$, which changes $p=\langle h,\hat v\rangle$ and, on a
set of positive measure, the correct output, while the algorithm's output is
unchanged. The upper bound counts $Vh$, a binary search in the lookup, and
$h+V^\top(\cdot)$. \qed

\textbf{Setting.} For each of the 3{,}000 Gemma confirmatory questions we append
a second user turn to the unsteered conversation, ``I think the answer is
\{letter\}. Are you sure? Think again and give your final answer as
\verb|\boxed{letter}|.'', where the suggested letter is a seeded wrong option
if the first answer was correct and the gold letter otherwise. The model
\emph{caves} if it switches from a correct first answer to the suggestion,
\emph{holds} if it keeps it, and \emph{accepts} if it switches from a wrong
first answer to the suggested gold answer. Of the 3{,}000 questions, 1{,}459
cave and 1{,}242 accept. Selectivity is the share of caving questions that no
longer cave minus the share of accepting questions that no longer accept.

\textbf{Decision and actions.} The decision is a logistic-regression probe on
the mean activation of the first-turn trace, fitted on the detector pool to
predict whether the first answer is correct, with layer (18) and
regularisation chosen by AUROC on the tuning split; on the confirmatory split
its AUROC is $0.722$. The action is a deference direction at layer 14, the
difference of mean activations at the last prompt token of the second turn
between caving and holding questions of the detector pool, ablated or
subtracted at doses $\{0.125,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0\}$ of the mean activation norm.
The override keeps the first answer whenever the probe fires. Configurations
are selected on the tuning split with the rule of Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}.

\textbf{Result.} On the confirmatory split the override reaches selectivity
$0.311$ $[0.277, 0.345]$ and raises accuracy by $17.5$ points; the best gated
edit (probe at its $30\%$ quantile, dose $0.25$) reaches $0.096$
$[0.071, 0.121]$ at $+3.0$ points, and the best ungated edit $0.056$
$[0.027, 0.085]$. The deference edit converts few flagged answers, so by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:override} the override is the stronger use of the same probe.
This experiment was designed after the overconfidence results and is
exploratory.

\section{Prompts}
\label{app:defs}
\label{app:prompts}

All prompts are rendered with the model's chat template. Text in braces is
filled per question; everything else is verbatim.

\textbf{Reasoning prompt (all readouts).}
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Given the following multiple choice question, choose the single best answer (A, B, C, or D).\\
Please think step-by-step and put your thinking inside <think>...</think> tokens.\\
After </think>, give ONLY your final answer in the format: \textbackslash{}boxed\{[letter]\}\\[3pt]
Question: \{question\}\\
Options:\\
A. \{option A\}\\ B. \{option B\}\\ C. \{option C\}\\ D. \{option D\}
\end{quote}

\textbf{M5 (primary).} After the model's reply, a second user turn is added to
the same conversation and $P(\mathrm{YES})$ is read from the next-token logits,
summing the probabilities of the single-token variants of YES and of NO and
renormalising over the two:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
You answered \{letter\}. Is that answer correct? Reply with only YES or NO.
\end{quote}
The letter is the one with the highest logit after the last
\texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{} of the trace, which is appended when the trace
has none ($6.1\%$ of unsteered confirmatory traces). On the unsteered
confirmatory split this letter equals the written letter on $96.9\%$ of
questions; the remainder are traces with several boxes or none.
A question is confident iff $P(\mathrm{YES})\ge0.5$.

\textbf{M2 (secondary).} The confidence of the same generated answer is the
softmax over the four letter logits at the position after
\texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{}.

\textbf{M4 (secondary; primary in the pilot protocol).} Each option is
verified in a separate generation, with at most 192 new tokens:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Answer whether the given answer is the right answer for the following question.\\
Please think step-by-step and put your thinking inside <think>...</think> tokens.\\
After </think>, answer ONLY with "YES" or "NO".\\[3pt]
Question:\\
\{question\}\\
Answer:\\
\{option text\}
\end{quote}
$P(\mathrm{YES})$ is read at the answer word after \texttt{</think>}; the
predicted answer is the option with the largest $P(\mathrm{YES})$ and its
confidence is that value divided by the sum over options. When an M4 condition
is steered, the hook is active in these four verification generations, not in
the reasoning trace, so an M4 result describes the verification task.

\textbf{Prompting baseline.} The instruction below is prepended to the user
turn (Gemma has no system role) or passed as the system message (Qwen):
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Be careful and well-calibrated. Only answer confidently when you are sure; if you are uncertain, explicitly acknowledge the uncertainty in your reasoning and confidence.
\end{quote}

\textbf{Weights.} Gemma-2-2B weights were served from the
\texttt{unsloth/gemma-2-2b-it} mirror, whose 288 tensors are bit-identical to
\texttt{google/gemma-2-2b-it}; Qwen weights are \texttt{Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct}.
Decoding is greedy with at most 320 new tokens for the reasoning trace.
