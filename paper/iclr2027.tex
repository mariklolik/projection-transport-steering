\title{Projection-Transport Steering:\\ What the Decision Buys in Conditional Activation Steering}
\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Conditional activation steering edits a language model's residual stream only
on inputs that a decision flags, and it is usually shown to beat global steering
at removing a behavior while sparing the rest. We introduce Projection-Transport
Steering (PTS), which separates a conditional edit into a decision and an
action, characterizes the minimal action local to a behavioral subspace as an
optimal-transport map, and accounts exactly for each part: selectivity equals
the decision's error rates weighted by the action's conversion rates on the
inputs it flags. The account yields a reference every conditional method should
beat, detecting the behavior and overriding its readout, and states when
steering can beat it. On answer-level overconfidence, in pre-registered
comparisons on two model families and two benchmarks, PTS is the most selective
method in one setting and level with or below the best in the other two. The
account explains each outcome: the decision determines what a gated edit can
reach, and detect-and-override with the same decision matches or exceeds the
best gated edit in all three.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Language models carry many behaviors along directions of their activations,
and activation steering changes a behavior by moving the activation along such
a direction~\citep{turner2023actadd,rimsky2024caa,zou2023repe,li2023iti,arditi2024refusal}.
The edit costs one vector operation per token and is applied to every input, so
a dose that removes a behavior also changes the inputs that never showed it.
Conditional methods place a decision in front of the edit:
CAST~\citep{lee2024cast} steers only when the prompt resembles a condition
vector, and PCHI~\citep{liu2026probe} rescales attention heads only when a
probe flags a wrong but confident answer. Both are evaluated against global
steering, and both improve on it.

Such comparisons do not say what the improvement comes from. A conditional edit
makes two choices, which inputs to edit and how to edit them, and a better
result can come from either. We study overconfidence in multiple-choice
reasoning, where the answers to change are the ones the model gets wrong while
stating that it is right, and the answers to protect are the ones it gets
right and says so. The additive edit that removes every overconfident-wrong
answer of \texttt{gemma-2-2b-it} also removes every confident-right one
(Sec.~\ref{ssec:confirm}), so the problem calls for a decision, and the
question is how much the decision, as opposed to the edit, is doing.

We introduce Projection-Transport Steering (PTS), which treats the two choices
separately. The action is the smallest edit that moves a projection of the
activation to a target law and leaves its orthogonal complement unchanged; it
is an optimal-transport map, and shifts, ablation and clamps are its instances.
The decision is a likelihood-ratio test on a statistic of the input. Our
analysis rests on an identity: the selectivity of any conditional edit equals
the decision's true-positive rate times the rate at which the action converts
the flagged target answers, minus its false-positive rate times the rate at
which it converts the flagged protected ones. The identity makes the
contribution of each choice measurable, and it supplies the reference a
conditional edit must beat: flag the behavior with the same decision and
override its readout, which converts every flagged answer and edits nothing.

Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item A selectivity identity for conditional edits, the condition under which
steering is more selective than detect-and-override, a corrected
Neyman--Pearson characterization of the optimal decision, and the
characterization of projection-local actions as transport maps
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).
\item A pre-registered comparison in which every method is tuned under one rule
and evaluated once on unseen questions, for two model families and a second
benchmark. PTS is the most selective method on Gemma-MMLU, significantly so
against seven baselines, level with the best methods on Qwen-MMLU, and below the
best two, not significantly, on Gemma-ARC (Sec.~\ref{ssec:confirm}).
\item Evidence that the decision determines the outcome. Across five
decisions, the override reference is ordered by the decision's held-out AUROC,
and gated edits reach it only when the action converts nearly every flagged
answer; on Qwen the override is more than twice as selective as any gated edit
(Sec.~\ref{ssec:decides}).
\item Evidence about where the edit acts: a shift acts mainly in the turn
where the model states its confidence and ablation mainly in the reasoning
trace (Sec.~\ref{ssec:where}); the decision can be taken from the prompt, so
the operator runs in a single pass on Gemma (Sec.~\ref{ssec:onepass-results}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Global steering.} ActAdd~\citep{turner2023actadd} and
CAA~\citep{rimsky2024caa} add a contrastive direction to the residual stream,
ITI~\citep{li2023iti} shifts probe-selected attention heads,
RepE~\citep{zou2023repe} reads and writes representation directions,
directional ablation~\citep{arditi2024refusal} projects a direction out, and
SAE clamping~\citep{templeton2024scaling} pins a feature. The same edit is
applied to every input, and AxBench~\citep{wu2025axbench} and studies of
steering reliability~\citep{tan2024generalisation,braun2025unreliability}
report that such edits are brittle across inputs. Table~\ref{tab:family}
places these operators in one family of transfer functions.

\textbf{Distribution matching.} MiMiC~\citep{singh2024mimic} derives the
Gaussian optimal-transport affine map between two activation distributions in
the full space, Linear-AcT~\citep{rodriguez2025act} transports each neuron
with a univariate map and a strength parameter, and
LinEAS~\citep{rodriguez2025lineas} learns affine maps end to end.
COAST~\citep{nguyen2026coast} characterises the covariance-weighted projection
that minimises collateral damage for a point target, and
A-LQR~\citep{nguyen2026alqr} treats steering as linear-quadratic control. These
results concern the action. Theorem~\ref{thm:ot} is the projection-local
version of the same transport argument, and we do not claim it as the source
of the empirical gains; what is new is its pairing with an exact account of
what a decision adds.

\textbf{Conditional steering.} CAST~\citep{lee2024cast} applies a behavior
vector only when the prompt's hidden state is similar to a condition vector,
and is the baseline closest to our design; we reimplement its prompt-level
condition and give it the same labelled data as our detector. Dynamic and
semantics-adaptive methods scale the edit per
token~\citep{ferrando2026dsas,wang2025sadi,scalena2024dynamic}, and
GAPS~\citep{gaps2026} gates individual dimensions. Closest in aim is
PCHI~\citep{liu2026probe}, which also separates detection from intervention
for verbalised overconfidence: a linear probe on the confidence template
conditions a learned rescaling of attention heads, reaching large
wrong-confident corrections at small damage on mathematics questions. PCHI
learns the action for a fixed readout position; we keep the action fixed and
ask what the decision contributes, when it can be taken, and how much it can
buy. The accounting identity and ceiling of Sec.~\ref{sec:method} apply to
PCHI as well. Probes read after a few generated tokens predict whether steering
will succeed~\citep{steerable2026}, detectors of a finished behavior forecast it
worse from a prefix~\citep{fpcg2026}, and reading a direction off already-edited
activations degrades it~\citep{luo2026yopo}; we measure the prefix cost
directly and place the reader above the actor. Our detect-and-override
reference is selective prediction with a correctness probe: that a model's
activations predict whether its answer is right is well
established~\citep{kadavath2022know,azaria2023lying}, and the identity makes it
the baseline a conditional edit of a confidence readout has to beat.

\section{Projection-Transport Steering}
\label{sec:method}

A conditional edit makes two choices: how to change an activation, and on which
inputs to change it. We call the first the \emph{action} and the second the
\emph{decision}. This section fixes the action by a displacement criterion
(Sec.~\ref{ssec:action}), writes selectivity as a function of the two choices
(Sec.~\ref{ssec:decision}), and describes how the decision is taken in a single
pass (Sec.~\ref{ssec:onepass}). Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\textbf{Setup.} Let $h\in\mathbb{R}^d$ be the residual-stream activation at a
fixed layer and $V\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}$ a matrix with orthonormal rows
that spans a behavioral subspace. We write $q=Vh$ for the projection, $\mu$ for
the law of $h$ under unsteered generation, $\mu_V$ for the law of $q$, and $\nu$
for a target law on $\mathbb{R}^k$. In our experiments $k=1$ and $V=\hat v^\top$
is the confidence direction, the difference of mean activations between
confident and nonconfident answers on an extraction split
(Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}).

\subsection{The action}
\label{ssec:action}

Large edits are what damage generation (Appendix~\ref{app:capability}), so we
ask for the smallest edit that moves the projection to a given target law and
leaves the rest of the activation alone.

\begin{definition}[projection-local steering]
\label{def:local}
An edit $T:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}^d$ is \emph{local} to $V$ if
$(I-V^\top V)\,T(h)=(I-V^\top V)\,h$, and it \emph{reaches} $\nu$ if $Vh'$ is
distributed as $\nu$ when $h\sim\mu$ and $h'=T(h)$. Its cost is
$C(T)=\mathbb{E}_\mu\lVert T(h)-h\rVert^2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[local steering is transport]
\label{thm:ot}
Let $\mu_V$ be absolutely continuous with finite second moment, and let $\nu$
have finite second moment. Among local edits that reach $\nu$, the cost is
minimised by
\begin{equation}
T(h)=h+V^\top\big(S(Vh)-Vh\big),
\label{eq:edit}
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the optimal-transport (Brenier) map from $\mu_V$ to $\nu$; the
minimum cost is $\Wtwo^2(\mu_V,\nu)$, and every minimiser agrees with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:edit} on the projection.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[what a transfer function can and cannot do]
\label{cor:additive}
(a) The shift $S(p)=p+\alpha$ is the optimal map iff $\nu$ is a translate of
$\mu_V$. (b) If two groups of inputs have the same law of $p$ and the event that
an input changes state depends on $h$ only through $p$, every transfer function
changes both groups at the same rate.
\end{corollary}

In words, once the subspace and the target are fixed, the only freedom left is
the one-dimensional map $S$, and the cheapest $S$ is the monotone
rearrangement of quantiles. Table~\ref{tab:family} lists the operators this
covers. Directional ablation is the map onto $\delta_0$, a clamp is the map
onto a truncated law, and the additive shift $S(p)=p+\alpha$ is the map onto a
shifted copy of $\mu_V$. A shift is optimal exactly when the target is a pure
translation (Cor.~\ref{cor:additive}); it is the member we end up selecting,
and the theorem says it spends no displacement off the confidence coordinate.

\subsection{The decision}
\label{ssec:decision}

A decision reads a statistic $s$ of the input and runs the action on the event
$\Gamma=\{s>\tau\}$; elsewhere the output is exactly the unsteered one. We
measure the result with selectivity,
$\mathrm{Sel}=P(\text{leave }\ocw\mid\ocw)-P(\text{leave }\crok\mid\crok)$, the
share of overconfident-wrong answers (\ocw{}) the edit removes minus the share
of confident-right answers (\crok{}) it destroys. Write $\mathrm{TPR}$ and
$\mathrm{FPR}$ for the rates at which the decision fires on \ocw{} and \crok{},
and $\rho_O$, $\rho_C$ for the rates at which the action changes the state of a
flagged \ocw{} and a flagged \crok{} answer.

\begin{proposition}[selectivity accounting]
\label{prop:account}
For any decision and action that leave unflagged outputs unchanged,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Sel}=\mathrm{TPR}\cdot\rho_O-\mathrm{FPR}\cdot\rho_C ,
\label{eq:account}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_O$ and $\rho_C$ are conversion rates among flagged answers.
\end{proposition}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:account} is an identity, and it separates the two choices. The
decision sets $(\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})$ and, through which answers it
flags, the conversion rates the action achieves on them. Two consequences
follow. First, if the action converts flagged and unflagged answers of the
same state at the same rate, $\rho_O$ and $\rho_C$ can be read off one ungated
run and the equation predicts the selectivity of every decision placed in
front of that action. Second, the identity contains its own reference point.
A decision that simply overrides the reported confidence of the answers it
flags converts every one of them, $\rho_O=\rho_C=1$, and reaches
$\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR}$. A gated edit is more selective than this
\emph{detect-and-override} reference exactly when
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{TPR}\,(1-\rho_O)<\mathrm{FPR}\,(1-\rho_C),
\label{eq:override}
\end{equation}
that is, when the action spares the confident-right answers the decision
flags by mistake more often than it fails on the overconfident-wrong answers
it flags correctly. The decision term is bounded by how well $s$ separates the
two states.

\begin{theorem}[optimal decision and its ceiling]
\label{thm:gate}
Suppose that within each state the action's conversion probability does not
depend on $s$. (a) For any objective increasing in TPR and decreasing in FPR,
including Eq.~\eqref{eq:account}, thresholding the likelihood ratio of $s$
between \ocw{} and \crok{} is optimal at every operating point. Without the
assumption, the optimal decision thresholds the conversion-weighted ratio
$p_O(s)\,r_O(s)/\big(p_C(s)\,r_C(s)\big)$, where $p$ is the class-conditional
density of $s$ and $r(s)$ the conversion probability given $s$. (b) If the
concave ROC of $s$ has area $\mathrm{AUC}$, then
$\max_\tau(\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR})\le2\,\mathrm{AUC}-1$, so the
detect-and-override reference is bounded by $2\,\mathrm{AUC}-1$, and so is any
gated edit with $\rho_O=\rho_C$.
\end{theorem}

We therefore implement the decision as a logistic-regression probe, an estimate
of the log-likelihood ratio, fitted on answers labelled by the evaluation
readout, and we judge it by its AUROC on questions it was not fitted to. The
same bound explains why the decision should not read the coordinate the action
edits. On held-out questions the confidence projection separates \ocw{} from
\crok{} at AUROC $0.64$ for Gemma and $0.53$ for Qwen, so a threshold on it buys
little, and an edit that depends only on that coordinate changes both states
at rates that differ only through the rest of the activation
(Cor.~\ref{cor:additive}).

\subsection{Deciding in one pass}
\label{ssec:onepass}

A decision that reads the finished answer requires generating every flagged
question twice. The decision can instead read the prompt, or the mean
activation over the first $t$ generated tokens, with a probe fitted on exactly
that statistic. Theorem~\ref{thm:gate} applies to any such statistic, so the
cost of deciding early is the AUROC lost relative to the finished trace. The
reader sits at or above the layer the action writes, the action starts at the decision
token, and positions written before it keep their cached keys. The operator
then costs two $d$-dimensional dot products per token.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp}\label{sec:data}

\textbf{Models and task.} The main model is \texttt{gemma-2-2b-it}
\citep{gemma2024}; the confirmatory comparison is repeated on
\texttt{Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct}, a second model family, with its own directions,
detector and tuning. Both reason inside \texttt{<think>...</think>} and answer
\verb|\boxed{letter}| on MMLU~\citep{hendrycks2021mmlu}; decoding is greedy.
The action is applied at layer 14 of 26 (Gemma) and 14 of 28 (Qwen). A
replication on ARC-Challenge~\citep{clark2018arc} reuses the Gemma directions,
refits every decision on ARC training questions, and follows the same protocol
with its own tuning and confirmatory splits (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\textbf{Splits.} All splits are disjoint sets of MMLU test questions (ids in
the released code): an \emph{extraction} split of 400 questions for the
directions and transport targets, a \emph{detector} pool of 4{,}000 for the
detector, a \emph{tuning} split of 1{,}200 on which every method's
configuration is chosen, and a \emph{confirmatory} split of 3{,}000 used once,
under a protocol frozen before its steered rollouts were generated
(Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\textbf{What is measured.} The primary readout (M5) scores the answer the
model actually generates. Correctness is the \verb|\boxed{}| letter against
the gold answer; confidence is $P(\mathrm{YES})$ when the model is then asked,
in the same conversation, whether that answer is correct. A steered question is
steered for both the reasoning trace and the confidence query, so the answer and
its confidence come from the same edited model. Each question is in one of four
states: \emph{overconfident-wrong} (\ocw{}, wrong with $P(\mathrm{YES})\ge0.5$),
nonconfident-wrong, nonconfident-right, and \emph{confident-right} (\crok{}).
Two secondary readouts, the letter-logit confidence of the same answer
and a per-option verification readout, are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}; the prompts are in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.

\textbf{Metrics.} Pairing each question with itself before and after the
edit, removal is $P(\text{not }\ocw\text{ after}\mid\ocw\text{ before})$ over
the $n_{\ocw}$ questions that started in \ocw{}, retention is
$P(\crok\text{ after}\mid\crok\text{ before})$ over the $n_{\crok}$ that
started in \crok{}, and selectivity is
$\mathrm{Sel}=\text{removal}-(1-\text{retention})$: $1$ for an edit that
removes every \ocw{} and keeps every \crok{}, $0$ for one that treats them
alike. $\Delta$acc is the change in accuracy over all questions, and ECE is
the 10-bin expected calibration error of the confidence.

\textbf{Methods and tuning.} We compare prompting (a calibration instruction),
additive CAA~\citep{rimsky2024caa}, directional
ablation~\citep{arditi2024refusal}, MiMiC~\citep{singh2024mimic},
Linear-AcT~\citep{rodriguez2025act} and two conditional shifts: CAST with its
original prompt condition, a cosine threshold to a condition vector computed
from the same labelled pool our detector uses~\citep{lee2024cast}, and a
CAST-style variant conditioned on the finished trace's projection onto the
extraction-split \ocw{}--\crok{} direction. All are reimplemented on the same
hooks, records and readouts. The PTS decision is a logistic-regression probe on
the mean activation of one layer, fitted on the detector pool, with its layer
and regularisation chosen by AUROC on the tuning split; the action is ablation
or a shift of the confidence coordinate. Each method searches a grid of 8 to 16
configurations on the tuning split, and one rule selects all of them: the
highest selectivity among configurations whose $\Delta$acc is at least $-0.01$
(Appendix~\ref{app:tuning}). Post-hoc PTS reads the finished unsteered answer
and regenerates the flagged questions; single-pass PTS decides at the prompt,
or after $t\in\{16,32,64,128\}$ generated tokens with a probe fitted on that
prefix, and acts from then on.

\textbf{Statistics.} The unit of analysis is the question. Intervals are
question-level paired bootstrap intervals with $10^4$ resamples; comparisons
between methods resample the same questions for both. The seven primary
comparisons of post-hoc PTS against the baselines are Holm-adjusted. We call
an accuracy change non-inferior when the lower end of its $95\%$ interval is
above $-0.02$, a margin fixed in the protocol.

\subsection{The selectivity identity predicts gated edits}
\label{ssec:law}

\textbf{The identity turns one ungated run into a prediction for every
decision.} On the Gemma tuning split we measure the conversion rates of six
actions once, compose each with two decisions at 20 thresholds, and compare
the prediction $\mathrm{TPR}\,\rho_O-\mathrm{FPR}\,\rho_C$ with the measured
selectivity (Fig.~\ref{fig:law}a). The two agree with correlation $0.97$ over
240 combinations, and with correlation $0.91$ over the 240 Qwen combinations,
whose selectivities span a narrower range; the prediction is higher by $0.033$
on average for Gemma and by $0.012$ for Qwen. The bias comes from the
independence assumption: the answers a decision flags are converted more often
than the average answer of their state, and more so in the protected state. With the flagged conversion rates in place of the
ungated ones the identity is exact. Because a post-hoc gated arm is, question by
question, either its unsteered or its ungated output, the gated selectivity can
be composed from one ungated run; on the confirmatory split the four gated arms
land within $0.010$ of that composition (Appendix~\ref{app:law}), which is how
we score gated configurations during tuning.

\textbf{The identity also says what each choice contributes.} The shift the
selection rule picks for Gemma, $S(p)=p-0.375\lVert h\rVert$, changes the state
of $84\%$ of overconfident-wrong and $59\%$ of confident-right answers when
applied everywhere. By Prop.~\ref{prop:account}, a decision recovers $0.59$ for
every confident-right answer it leaves alone and forfeits $0.84$ for every
overconfident-wrong answer it misses, so the value of detection grows with how
indiscriminate the action is. The probe reads the unsteered answer at held-out
AUROC $0.74$, against $0.67$ for the direction that conditions the CAST-style
baseline (Fig.~\ref{fig:law}b); at its selected threshold it fires on $54\%$ of
overconfident-wrong and $22\%$ of confident-right answers.

answer and every confident-right one, at a cost of $15.3$ accuracy points;
smaller doses keep more of both (Appendix~\ref{app:tuning}). The tuned dose,
$p-0.25\lVert h\rVert$, reaches selectivity $0.197$ there.

\textbf{The override reference is ordered by the decision, and gated edits

and the frozen PTS arm $0.294$. On Qwen the selected action is ablation, which

protocol and is not pre-registered.

\subsection{Where the edit acts}
\label{ssec:where}

\textbf{Which part of generation the edit changes depends on the action.} We
reran the frozen PTS decision with the action applied only during the reasoning
trace, or only during the turn in which the model states its confidence. On
Gemma, where the action is a shift, steering only the confidence turn reaches
selectivity $0.274$ $[0.237, 0.312]$, close to the $0.294$ of the full edit,
and steering only the trace reaches $0.159$. On Qwen, where the action is
ablation, the pattern reverses: the trace-only edit reaches $0.172$, equal to the
full edit, and the confidence-turn edit $0.006$. A shift of the confidence
coordinate therefore acts mainly by lowering the stated confidence directly,
while ablation acts through the reasoning. The steered traces also change
answers: on Gemma PTS turns $124$ of the $963$ overconfident-wrong answers into
correct ones and $106$ of the $1{,}399$ confident-right answers into wrong ones
(Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}).

\subsection{Deciding in one pass}
\label{ssec:onepass-results}

\textbf{Most of the detection is available before the answer is written.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:law}b plots the held-out AUROC of a probe fitted on each
statistic. For Gemma the prompt alone reaches $0.720$ and the first 16 tokens
$0.720$, against $0.741$ for the finished answer; for Qwen the prompt reaches
$0.761$ against $0.785$. On Gemma the single-pass operators in
Table~\ref{tab:confirm} match the post-hoc one: deciding at token 32 gives selectivity $0.294$ and
deciding at the prompt $0.306$, with paired differences to the post-hoc
decision of $0.000$ and $+0.012$. On Qwen the gap in AUROC is larger, and the
selected single-pass variants reach $0.109$ (token 16) and $0.120$ (prompt),
below the post-hoc $0.172$. A decision at the prompt costs one dot
product on an activation the forward pass already computes, so the operator
adds no generation; in an end-to-end measurement of the earlier prefix gate the
single-pass operator ran at $1.05\times$ the unsteered wall clock, against
$1.97\times$ for regenerating flagged answers (Appendix~\ref{app:legacy}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented Projection-Transport Steering, which splits a conditional
activation edit into a decision and an action and accounts exactly for each.
The accounting has a practical consequence for how conditional steering is
evaluated. A gated edit should be compared with global steering and with
detect-and-override under the same decision, and the identity says in advance
when it can beat the latter: only if the action spares the protected answers the
decision flags by mistake more often than it misses the target answers it flags
correctly. In our experiments the decision determined the outcome, the best
decision was a probe trained on held-out labels, and overriding the readout
with it was as selective as steering on one model and far more selective on the
other.

Our study has limitations. The evidence covers one behavior, measured with one
confidence readout, on two benchmarks and two instruction-tuned models; an
earlier form of the operator passed on one of three models on TruthfulQA
(Appendix~\ref{app:postreview}). The decision is a probe trained on labelled
answers, and its threshold is tied to the distribution it was fitted on
(Appendices~\ref{app:capability} and~\ref{app:transfer}). The override
reference applies only where the behavior has a readout that can be
overwritten; for behaviors expressed in free text, the conversion rates of the
action become the whole question.

\section{Confirmatory protocol}
\label{app:protocol}

The protocol was committed to the repository before any steered rollout on the
confirmatory split was generated (\texttt{rebuttal/protocols/confirmatory-v19.md},
with two amendments made after the tuning pass and before launch), together
with the SHA-256 hashes of the selected configurations and of every fitted
detector (\texttt{confirmatory-v19-gemma.sha256}).

\textbf{Splits.} All splits are subsets of the 14{,}042-question MMLU test set,
identified by index and constructed by \texttt{label\_pool.split\_records}. The
extraction split (400) excludes the first evaluation subset of the earlier
version; the tuning split (1{,}200) excludes the extraction split and the five
earlier evaluation subsets; the detector pool (4{,}000) and the confirmatory
split (3{,}000) are consecutive blocks of a seeded permutation of the
remaining questions. No question appears in two of the four splits.

\textbf{Selection rule.} For every method the configuration with the highest
tuning-split selectivity among those with $\Delta\mathrm{acc}\ge-0.01$ is
carried unchanged to the confirmatory split. Gated configurations are scored on
the tuning split by combining the ungated rollouts of the same split with the
gate's flags (Prop.~\ref{prop:account} makes this exact up to batched-decoding
nondeterminism; Appendix~\ref{app:law}); single-pass configurations are real
runs. Every confirmatory number is a real run.

\textbf{Tests.} Primary: PTS post-hoc against each of the seven baselines on
pooled M5 selectivity, question-level paired bootstrap with $10^4$ resamples,
two-sided, Holm-adjusted over the seven comparisons. Accuracy: an arm is
non-inferior if the lower end of the $95\%$ interval of its accuracy change is
above $-0.02$. The single-pass and prompt-decision variants, M4 and M2
selectivities, removal, retention and ECE are secondary and reported without
a claim.

\textbf{Released records.} Every unsteered and steered answer of the tuning and
confirmatory splits is released with its state, confidence and correctness
(\texttt{results/release/records.csv.gz}); \texttt{paper/recompute\_from\_records.py}
recomputes each selectivity and paired interval in Table~\ref{tab:confirm} from
that file alone.

\textbf{ARC replication.} A second protocol
(\texttt{confirmatory-v20-arc.md}), frozen before any steered ARC rollout,
repeats the Gemma comparison on ARC-Challenge: a detector pool of 1{,}012 and a
tuning split of 400 from the ARC training and validation questions, and the
1{,}165-question test split as the confirmatory split. The directions, moments
and action layer are those fitted on MMLU; every decision is refitted on the ARC
pool together with the MMLU pool, and every configuration is selected on the
ARC tuning split with the same rule and grids.

\textbf{Analyses added after the protocol.} The detect-and-override reference,
the difference-of-means decision fitted on 4{,}400 labels, the decision-by-action
matrix (Sec.~\ref{ssec:decides}), the trace-only and confidence-turn-only edits
(Sec.~\ref{ssec:where}), calibration and the out-of-domain negatives were run
after the confirmatory results were known. They use the same splits and the
frozen decisions, and we report them as exploratory.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\textbf{Theorem~\ref{thm:ot}.} By locality, $T(h)-h\in\mathrm{span}(V^\top)$
$\mu$-a.s., hence $\lVert T(h)-h\rVert^2=\lVert VT(h)-Vh\rVert^2$ because the
rows of $V$ are orthonormal, and $C(T)=\mathbb{E}\lVert VT(h)-Vh\rVert^2$. The
joint law $\gamma$ of $(Vh,VT(h))$ is a coupling of $(\mu_V,\nu)$ by the goal
constraint, so $C(T)=\int\lVert y-x\rVert^2d\gamma\ge\Wtwo^2(\mu_V,\nu)$.
Because $\mu_V$ is absolutely continuous and both laws have finite second
moments, Brenier's theorem gives a unique optimal coupling, induced by
$S=\nabla\varphi$ with $\varphi$ convex. $T^*(h)=h+V^\top(S(Vh)-Vh)$ is
admissible ($VV^\top=I_k$ gives $VT^*(h)=S(Vh)$, and the complement is
untouched) and attains the bound. For any minimiser the induced coupling is
optimal, hence equal to the unique one, so $VT(h)=S(Vh)$ a.s.\ \qed

For $k=1$ absolute continuity can be weakened to atomlessness of $\mu_V$: the
monotone rearrangement $F_\nu^{-1}\circ F_{\mu_V}$ is then an optimal map for
every $\nu$ with a finite second moment, including targets with atoms such as
$\delta_0$ (ablation) or a law truncated at $c$ (clamp); uniqueness of the map
can fail only on a $\mu_V$-null set.

\begin{proposition}[metric covariance]
\label{prop:metric}
If the cost is $(\Delta h)^\top M(\Delta h)$ with $M\succ0$, the admissible
problem is optimal transport of $\mu_V$ to $\nu$ in $\mathbb{R}^k$ under the
cost $\lVert\cdot\rVert^2_{M_V}$, $M_V=VMV^\top\succ0$, and the optimal
operator is $T^*(h)=h+V^\top(S_M(Vh)-Vh)$ with
$S_M=M_V^{-1/2}\circ\nabla\varphi\circ M_V^{1/2}$, $\varphi$ convex.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof.} Locality writes $\Delta h=V^\top a$ with $a\in\mathbb{R}^k$, and
the goal constraint with $VV^\top=I_k$ fixes $a=S(Vh)-Vh$ for the map $S$ in
use. Then $\lVert\Delta h\rVert_M^2=a^\top M_Va$. The cost is a latent-space
Mahalanobis cost on $\mathbb{R}^k$; it is not the full-space Mahalanobis
distance of $\Delta h$ unless $M$ commutes with $V^\top V$. In the coordinates
$z=M_V^{1/2}q$ it is Euclidean, the change of variables is internal to
$\mathbb{R}^k$, and Brenier's theorem in $z$ (the pushforward of an absolutely
continuous law under an invertible linear map is absolutely continuous) gives
$\nabla\varphi$; mapping back gives $S_M$. \qed

\textbf{Corollary~\ref{cor:additive}.} (a) If $\nu=\mu_V*\delta_\alpha$ the
map $x\mapsto x+\alpha$ is the gradient of a convex function pushing $\mu_V$
to $\nu$, hence the Brenier map. Conversely, if the optimal map is
$x+\alpha$ then $\nu$ is its pushforward, the shift. For one-dimensional
Gaussians the monotone map is $x\mapsto m_t+(\sigma_t/\sigma_s)(x-m_s)$, with
slope $\sigma_t/\sigma_s$. (b) Let $E$ be the event that a trace changes state
under the edit and suppose $P(E\mid h)=g(p,S(p))$. For subpopulation $A$,
$P(E\mid A)=\mathbb{E}[g(p,S(p))\mid A]=\int g(p,S(p))\,d\mu^A_p$, and the same
for $B$. If $\mu^A_p=\mu^B_p$ the two integrals coincide. \qed

The premise of (b) is a conditional-independence assumption: given $p$, the
state change must not depend on the complement $h_\perp$ or on the
subpopulation. It is the premise, not the conclusion, that the data reject
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).

\textbf{Proposition~\ref{prop:account}.} Let $R$ be the event that an \ocw{}
trace leaves \ocw{} and $L$ the event that a \crok{} trace leaves \crok{}. Off
$\Gamma$ the output equals the unsteered output, which is in its original state
by definition, so $R\subseteq\Gamma$ and $L\subseteq\Gamma$ up to null sets.
Then $P(R\mid\ocw)=P(\Gamma\mid\ocw)P(R\mid\ocw,\Gamma)=\mathrm{TPR}\,\rho_O$, and
likewise $P(L\mid\crok)=\mathrm{FPR}\,\rho_C$; subtracting gives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:account}. If, within \ocw{}, the indicator that the action
converts the trace is independent of $\Gamma$, then
$\rho_O=P(R^{\mathrm{ungated}}\mid\ocw)$, and likewise for \crok{}; the ungated
selectivity is $\rho_O-\rho_C$, and the difference is
$\rho_C(1-\mathrm{FPR})-\rho_O(1-\mathrm{TPR})$. \qed

The identity requires that the unflagged output be exactly the unsteered one.
The post-hoc gate satisfies this by construction. The single-pass gate
satisfies it for traces on which it never fires, since the action hook is the
identity until the decision token.

\textbf{Theorem~\ref{thm:gate}.} Write $p_O,p_C$ for the densities of $s$ on
\ocw{} and \crok{} answers and $r_O(s),r_C(s)$ for the probabilities that the
action converts an answer of each state with statistic $s$. For a decision
region $\Gamma$, $\mathrm{TPR}\,\rho_O=\int_\Gamma p_O r_O$ and
$\mathrm{FPR}\,\rho_C=\int_\Gamma p_C r_C$, so
$\mathrm{Sel}(\Gamma)=\int_\Gamma(p_O r_O-p_C r_C)$. Among regions with a given
value of $\int_\Gamma p_C r_C$, the Neyman--Pearson lemma applied to the
nonnegative measures $p_O r_O$ and $p_C r_C$ shows that
$\int_\Gamma p_O r_O$ is maximised by a superlevel set of their ratio, which is
the general statement in (a). If $r_O$ and $r_C$ are constant in $s$, the ratio
is a constant multiple of $p_O/p_C$, so every objective increasing in TPR and
decreasing in FPR is maximised on the likelihood-ratio ROC curve, which is (a).
The assumption matters: with $s\in\{a,b\}$, $p_O(a)=p_O(b)=\tfrac12$,
$p_C(a)=\tfrac14$, $p_C(b)=\tfrac34$, $r_O(a)=0.2$, $r_O(b)=1$, $r_C(a)=1$ and
$r_C(b)=0$, the likelihood ratio ranks $a$ above $b$, yet $\Gamma=\{b\}$ gives
selectivity $0.5$, against $-0.15$ for $\{a\}$ and $0.35$ for $\{a,b\}$. On
our data $r$ does depend on $s$: flagged answers are converted more often than
unflagged answers of the same state (Appendix~\ref{app:law}). Our detector is a
logistic-regression probe, a discriminative estimate of the log-likelihood
ratio up to the prior term; (a) states what the probe approximates, not that
the fitted probe is optimal. (b) The concavified ROC contains the path
$(0,0)\to(\mathrm{FPR}^*,\mathrm{TPR}^*)\to(1,1)$ through the Youden-optimal
point, whose area is $\tfrac12(1+J)$, so $J\le2\,\mathrm{AUC}-1$. The override
reference has selectivity $\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR}\le J$, and with
$\rho_O=\rho_C=\rho$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:account} gives $\mathrm{Sel}=\rho(\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR})\le J$. \qed

\begin{proposition}[transport under a fixed decision]
\label{prop:joint}
Among interventions local to $V$, equal to the identity off $\Gamma$, and whose
projections on $\Gamma$ follow $\nu_\Gamma$, the cost-minimal one applies the
Brenier map of $(\mu_V\mid\Gamma)\to\nu_\Gamma$ on $\Gamma$, at cost
$P(\Gamma)\,\Wtwo^2(\mu_V\!\mid\!\Gamma,\nu_\Gamma)$, provided
$\mu_V(\cdot\mid\Gamma)$ is absolutely continuous.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof.} Split $C(T)$ over $\Gamma$ and $\Gamma^c$. The second term is
zero by the identity constraint; the first, with the conditional goal
constraint, is Theorem~\ref{thm:ot} for $\mu(\cdot\mid\Gamma)$. \qed

This result fixes the decision. It does not say that a threshold gate is the
best decision for the displacement-priced objective; the next statement shows
that it generally is not, and when it is.

\begin{proposition}[graded dose]
\label{prop:dose}
Let $T_\lambda(h)=h+\lambda(s)\,V^\top(S(Vh)-Vh)$ with a dose
$\lambda:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]$ measurable in the detector statistic $s$. Write
$\pi(s)=P(\ocw\mid s)$, $d(s)=\mathbb{E}[\lVert S(Vh)-Vh\rVert^2\mid s]>0$, and
suppose the behavioral gain is linear in the dose, with slope $\beta_1>0$ on
\ocw{} and $-\beta_0<0$ on \crok{}. The dose maximising gain minus
displacement priced at $1/(2c)$ is
\[
\lambda^*(s)=\Big[\tfrac{c\,(\beta_0+\beta_1)\,(\pi(s)-\tau)}{d(s)}\Big]_0^1,\qquad
\tau=\tfrac{\beta_0}{\beta_0+\beta_1}.
\]
It is zero exactly on $\{\pi\le\tau\}$ and nondecreasing in the ratio
$(\pi(s)-\tau)/d(s)$. It is nondecreasing in $\pi$ when $d$ is constant, but
not in general. The threshold gate $\mathbf{1}[\pi>\tau]$ is optimal iff
$\lambda^*\in\{0,1\}$ almost surely, and strictly suboptimal iff
$P(0<\lambda^*<1)>0$.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof.} The displacement of $T_\lambda$ is
$\lambda(s)^2\lVert S(Vh)-Vh\rVert^2$, with expectation
$\mathbb{E}_s[\lambda(s)^2d(s)]$; the gain is
$\mathbb{E}_s[\lambda(s)((\beta_0+\beta_1)\pi(s)-\beta_0)]$. The objective is
an integral over $s$ of a strictly concave quadratic in $\lambda$, maximised
pointwise on $[0,1]$ by the clipped stationary point. It is the unique
maximiser a.s., so the indicator is optimal iff it agrees with $\lambda^*$
a.s., i.e.\ iff $\lambda^*\in\{0,1\}$ a.s.; otherwise the objective is strictly
larger at $\lambda^*$ on a set of positive measure. For non-monotonicity in
$\pi$, take $s_1,s_2$ with $\pi(s_1)=0.6<\pi(s_2)=0.7$, $\tau=0.5$,
$c(\beta_0+\beta_1)=1$, $d(s_1)=0.2$ and $d(s_2)=1$: then
$\lambda^*(s_1)=0.5>\lambda^*(s_2)=0.2$. For a counterexample to strict
suboptimality at every finite price, let $\pi$ take only the values $0.1$ and
$0.9$ with $\tau=0.5$ and $d\le0.4\,c(\beta_0+\beta_1)$: then
$\lambda^*\in\{0,1\}$ a.s.\ and the threshold gate is optimal. \qed

\textbf{Single-pass decision.} Let $\mathcal{F}_t$ be generated by the first $t$
generated tokens and $z_t$ the mean detector feature over them. A decision
measurable with respect to $\sigma(z_t)$ is a test between the
class-conditional laws of $z_t$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:gate} applies with $z_t$
in place of $s$ and nothing is assumed about how $z_t$ relates to the finished
trace. The reader hook sits at a layer at or above the actor; within one
forward pass the actor at position $t$ sees the decision accumulated over
positions $<t$, the running mean needs one sum and one counter per sequence,
and an edit at position $t$ changes only the keys and values written at $t$.
An earlier version of this paper also stated a time-uniform guarantee for a
running-mean gate; it assumed the token contributions form a sub-Gaussian
martingale-difference sequence around the finished-trace statistic, which we
cannot verify for this statistic, and we no longer use it.

\begin{proposition}[computational cost]
\label{prop:compute}
Any algorithm implementing a non-degenerate intervention local to a subspace
spanned by a dense direction reads all $d$ coordinates of $h$, so it costs
$\Omega(d)$ per token. Eq.~\eqref{eq:edit} costs $\Theta(kd+k\log K)$ with a
$K$-knot lookup, and the detector adds one $d$-dimensional dot product per
token.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof.} If a coordinate $i$ with $(\hat v)_i\neq0$ is never read, an
adversary perturbs $h_i$, which changes $p=\langle h,\hat v\rangle$ and, on a
set of positive measure, the correct output, while the algorithm's output is
unchanged. The upper bound counts $Vh$, a binary search in the lookup, and
$h+V^\top(\cdot)$. \qed

\section{Prompts}
\label{app:defs}
\label{app:prompts}

All prompts are rendered with the model's chat template. Text in braces is
filled per question; everything else is verbatim.

\textbf{Reasoning prompt (all readouts).}
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Given the following multiple choice question, choose the single best answer (A, B, C, or D).\\
Please think step-by-step and put your thinking inside <think>...</think> tokens.\\
After </think>, give ONLY your final answer in the format: \textbackslash{}boxed\{[letter]\}\\[3pt]
Question: \{question\}\\
Options:\\
A. \{option A\}\\ B. \{option B\}\\ C. \{option C\}\\ D. \{option D\}
\end{quote}

\textbf{M5 (primary).} After the model's reply, a second user turn is added to
the same conversation and $P(\mathrm{YES})$ is read from the next-token logits,
summing the probabilities of the single-token variants of YES and of NO and
renormalising over the two:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
You answered \{letter\}. Is that answer correct? Reply with only YES or NO.
\end{quote}
The letter is the one with the highest logit after the last
\texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{} of the trace, which is appended when the trace
has none ($6.1\%$ of unsteered confirmatory traces). On the unsteered
confirmatory split this letter equals the written letter on $96.9\%$ of
questions; the remainder are traces with several boxes or none.
A question is confident iff $P(\mathrm{YES})\ge0.5$.

\textbf{M2 (secondary).} The confidence of the same generated answer is the
softmax over the four letter logits at the position after
\texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{}.

\textbf{M4 (secondary; primary in the earlier version).} Each option is
verified in a separate generation, with at most 192 new tokens:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Answer whether the given answer is the right answer for the following question.\\
Please think step-by-step and put your thinking inside <think>...</think> tokens.\\
After </think>, answer ONLY with "YES" or "NO".\\[3pt]
Question:\\
\{question\}\\
Answer:\\
\{option text\}
\end{quote}
$P(\mathrm{YES})$ is read at the answer word after \texttt{</think>}; the
predicted answer is the option with the largest $P(\mathrm{YES})$ and its
confidence is that value divided by the sum over options. When an M4 condition
is steered, the hook is active in these four verification generations, not in
the reasoning trace, so an M4 result describes the verification task.

\textbf{Prompting baseline.} The instruction below is prepended to the user
turn (Gemma has no system role) or passed as the system message (Qwen):
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Be careful and well-calibrated. Only answer confidently when you are sure; if you are uncertain, explicitly acknowledge the uncertainty in your reasoning and confidence.
\end{quote}

\textbf{Weights.} Gemma-2-2B weights were served from the
\texttt{unsloth/gemma-2-2b-it} mirror, whose 288 tensors are bit-identical to
\texttt{google/gemma-2-2b-it}; Qwen weights are \texttt{Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct}.
Decoding is greedy with at most 320 new tokens for the reasoning trace.
